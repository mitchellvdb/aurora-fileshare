How to send a 50 GB file for free.

Fifty gigabytes is where most transfer services stop being free, and where several stop working at all. Here is what actually goes wrong at that size, and what to do instead.

Fifty gigabytes is not just ten times five

A 5 GB transfer and a 50 GB transfer fail in different ways. The small one is bounded by policy — some limit written into a pricing page. The large one starts running into physics, and into how browsers and networks actually behave over long periods.

Three things change at that size: the time the transfer occupies, the memory it needs while in flight, and the number of chances it has to be interrupted.

The upload arithmetic

On any service that stores your file, the bytes cross the network twice: once from you up to the server, and once from the server down to whoever you sent it to. Only the first leg runs on your upload connection, which on most consumer lines is the far slower half.

What that means for 50 GB, before the recipient has downloaded anything at all:

  • At 10 Mbit/s upload — roughly 11 hours
  • At 40 Mbit/s — roughly 3 hours
  • At 100 Mbit/s — a little over an hour

Those hours are the real cost of the upload step, and they are why "just use a transfer site" stops being good advice somewhere around this size. Your connection is occupied the whole time, and the recipient cannot begin until you have finished.

What tends to break

  • Memory. Tools that assemble a file in the browser before saving it need to hold the whole thing in RAM. At 50 GB that simply fails on most machines.
  • Interruptions. An eleven-hour upload has eleven hours in which a laptop can sleep, a connection can drop, or a session can time out. Without resumable uploads, an interruption at hour nine means starting again.
  • Free-tier ceilings. Most stop well below this, so the practical answer often becomes a subscription rather than a workaround.

Sending it directly instead

The alternative is to skip the middle entirely. Two browsers connect to each other and the file crosses the network once, from your machine to theirs. There is no upload step to wait through, because there is nothing to upload to — the recipient starts receiving as soon as they open the link.

Speed then depends on the slower of the two connections and the route between them, rather than on a service's capacity. For a concrete figure: between two gigabit connections we measured about 50 MB/s, and a real 39 GB transfer finished in fifteen to twenty minutes. Slower connections take proportionally longer, but the shape holds — one leg instead of two.

Aurora FileShare works this way, which is why it has no size limit to quote. There is no storage quota to hit because nothing is stored.

What you need for a transfer this size

  • Both of you online at the same time. Your device is the source, so the transfer happens while you are both there.
  • The sending tab left open. Closing it ends the share.
  • Free disk space on the receiving end. Fifty gigabytes has to land somewhere.
  • A current browser over HTTPS. That allows the download to stream straight to disk rather than being assembled in memory first, which is the difference between a 50 GB file working and failing.

When to do something else

Direct transfer is not always the right answer, and it is worth being straight about when it is not:

  • If the recipient is not available now, you need something that holds the file and waits. That is what paid transfer tiers are for, and the fee is the honest price of the disk.
  • If you are moving several terabytes, a physical disk in the post is still faster than almost any connection.
  • If you need a link that keeps working for months, you want hosting, not a transfer.

The short version

Pick your files, send the link you get back, and keep the tab open while the transfer runs. Nothing is uploaded first, so a 50 GB file starts moving in the same second a 5 MB one would. If it is useful, the FAQ covers passwords, expiry and the cases where a direct connection cannot be established.
