What "free" actually means on file transfer sites.

Every free service is paid for by something. Working out which model you are using tells you what you are actually giving up in exchange.

Free is a business model, not the absence of one

Storing a stranger's file costs money — the disk it occupies, the bandwidth in, the bandwidth out again, and the staff keeping it running. When a service does that without charging you, the money is arriving from somewhere else. There are only a few places it usually comes from.

The four common models

1. The free tier as a trial

By far the most common. The free allowance is marketing spend: large enough to prove the product works, small enough that anyone with a real need runs into the edge of it. The size ceiling and the expiry window are product decisions rather than technical ones — they are set where they produce upgrades.

Nothing about this is underhanded. It is worth recognising for what it is, though: you are not using a free product, you are using a demonstration of a paid one.

2. Advertising

The service is funded by attention rather than subscriptions, which means it needs to know things about visitors to sell that attention well. In practice that means tracking scripts, cookies and some degree of profile-building. Reasonable for a great many sites; worth a moment's thought before routing something sensitive through one.

3. The metadata

Less common and usually described in the terms rather than on the landing page. Even a service that never opens a file knows who sent it, roughly how big it was, when, and to whom it went. That pattern has commercial value on its own, independent of contents.

4. It genuinely costs almost nothing to run

This one is only available to a narrow set of designs. If a service never stores your file, it never pays for the disk or the bandwidth that dominate everyone else's costs — so there is very little to recover, and no particular reason to put a meter on it.

Telling which one you are using

  • Is there a paid tier? If so, every limit on the free one is a deliberate choice about where to place the upgrade, not a law of nature.
  • What does the upgrade prompt appear for? Whatever it interrupts is the thing being sold. Size, speed, expiry, or removing an advert.
  • Read the privacy policy for metadata, not just contents. "We never look at your files" and "we retain records of transfers" can both be true at once.
  • Look for what happens when you stop. A free product behaves the same on day one and day four hundred. A trial does not.

What "we delete your files" can mean

This phrase does a lot of work and is worth reading precisely. It can mean the download link stops resolving. It can mean the stored object is removed. Backups and operational logs may run on their own schedule, and often a record that the transfer happened outlives the file itself.

Most services disclose all of this somewhere. The point is not that anybody is lying — it is that "deleted" describes several different things, and only the policy tells you which one you got.

Our own model, stated plainly

It would be poor form to write all of the above and stay vague about ourselves.

Aurora FileShare is the fourth kind. Files travel directly between the two browsers, so we never store one and never pay to move one. Our server only introduces the two ends to each other and then steps aside, which costs little enough that metering it would be more trouble than it is worth. That is the whole reason there is no size limit and no account.

What we do see, because being precise matters more than sounding good: the server briefly holds file names, sizes and types in memory so the recipient knows what they are being offered, and it sees the IP addresses connecting to it, as every web server does, which it uses to rate limit. None of it is written to disk. There is a tip button if the tool saved you something, and it is run by Aurora Hosting — so this page is, in part, a shop window. That seems fairer to say than to leave you guessing.

The trade-off we make instead

Every model costs the user something. Ours costs convenience: because nothing is stored, both people have to be online at the same time, and the sender has to keep a tab open until the transfer finishes. If you need a file to sit and wait for someone, a storage-based service is genuinely the better tool and its fee is the honest price of the disk.
