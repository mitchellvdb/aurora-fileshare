Guide

Send large files without an account.

Most transfer services want you to sign up before they will move anything substantial. Here is why, what a free tier usually means, and how a transfer that never touches a server avoids the question entirely.

Why transfer services have limits at all

When you upload a file to a transfer service, that file has to live somewhere. A copy sits on a disk in a data centre from the moment your upload finishes until the moment it expires. Someone pays for that disk, for the bandwidth in, for the bandwidth out again, and for keeping the whole arrangement running.

That cost scales with size and with time. A service that let anonymous visitors park fifty gigabytes indefinitely, for nothing, would be paying real money for every stranger who passed through. So limits appear — not out of meanness, but because storage is the product and the product costs money.

Nearly every restriction you meet on a free tier traces back to that one fact.

The usual shape of a free tier

The specifics vary between services, but the patterns repeat:

  • A size ceiling. Free transfers commonly stop after a few gigabytes. That is roughly where storage cost stops being a rounding error.
  • An expiry window. Links often die after a few days. The shorter the window, the less disk is occupied at any moment — it is a cost control before it is a feature.
  • An account wall. Larger transfers, longer expiry and repeat use tend to require registration. An account gives the service something durable to attach usage to, and an address to reach you at later.
  • A retention policy that is not quite deletion. "Deleted after seven days" usually describes the download link. Backups and logs may follow their own schedule. This is normal and generally disclosed — it is just worth reading rather than assuming.

None of that is dishonest. It is the arithmetic of storing other people's data. But it does mean "free" often describes a trial of a paid product rather than a complete one.

The other approach: do not store it

There is a second way to move a file, which sidesteps the arithmetic rather than paying it.

Two browsers can connect directly to one another. Once they have, the bytes travel from one machine to the other without passing through anything in between. Nothing is uploaded, because there is nowhere to upload it to.

A server is still involved, but only briefly, and only to introduce the two browsers to each other — passing along the connection details each one needs in order to find the other. That step is called signalling. It moves a few kilobytes and then steps out of the way. It never receives the file.

That is how Aurora FileShare works, and it is why there is no size limit: there is no storage quota to hit, because there is no storage.

What that changes, and what it costs

The advantages follow directly. There is no upload step, so a large transfer starts moving immediately instead of after an hour of waiting. There is no size ceiling. There is no account, because there is nothing to attach one to. There is no retention policy, because once the transfer finishes the only copies that exist are the sender's original and the recipient's download.

The trade-offs follow just as directly, and they are real:

  • Both people have to be online at once. The sender's device is the source. This is a transfer, not a drop box — you cannot send a file to someone who is asleep and have it waiting for them.
  • The sender keeps a tab open. Closing it ends the share, the same way unplugging a server would.
  • Some networks refuse the direct route. Restrictive corporate and mobile networks can block it in a way the browser cannot work around. If both ends are on such a network the transfer cannot start; moving either end to a different connection usually fixes it.

If you need a file to sit somewhere and wait, a storage-based service is the right tool, and its limits are the honest price of that. If you need to hand something large to a person who is there now, not storing it is faster and cheaper for everyone involved.

What to check before trusting any transfer service

Whichever kind you use, the same few questions tell you most of what you need to know:

  • Where does the file physically go, and for how long? A specific answer is a good sign. Vagueness is not.
  • Encrypted to whom? "Encrypted" frequently means encrypted between you and the server — which still leaves the server able to read it. End to end means something stronger.
  • What is recorded? Almost everything records something. The useful question is what, and for how long.
  • Is the free tier complete, or a trial? The two look identical on a landing page.

Our own answers are on the FAQ, including the less flattering parts: the server does briefly hold file sizes in memory, and it does see the IP addresses connecting to it, as any web server does. We would rather write that down than round it off to "nothing".
